Case study 02 · Procurement analytics

Which suppliers are putting production at risk?

01 · Context

A blending plant can't run without its base oil

Lubricants are mostly base oil, with a smaller share of additive packages, then bottled and labelled. If one of those inputs arrives late, the blend schedule moves, and sometimes a customer order moves with it. The purchasing team manages around 40 suppliers across Singapore, Malaysia, China, Korea and Europe, and most of the inbound value comes by sea.

Before this report, delivery performance was checked supplier by supplier in the ERP. There was no single number the team agreed on, and no easy way to see whether a bad month was one supplier or a wider problem.

So the first job was agreeing on definitions. These are the rules I wrote into the model:

On timeSupplier's confirmed ETA lands within ±7 days of the date we requested.
In fullConfirmed quantity is within ±5% of the ordered quantity.
OTIFBoth of the above on the same PO line. No confirmed date counts as a fail.

The score is measured at PO line level, because one order can carry several materials with different dates.

03 · Finding one

73% or 47%? It depends on what you count

The usual way to report OTIF is to count lines: 1,501 of 2,061 lines passed, so 72.8%. That is a fair measure of how reliable suppliers are on an average order. But a US$5,000 drum of additive and a US$600,000 base oil shipment count the same.

Weighting each line by its value tells a different story. In January the two measures were close. From March onwards they split, and by August only 39% of the value due that month was confirmed on time and in full.

The line count hides the problem because the suppliers with the most lines are small-ticket additive suppliers who mostly deliver well. The big-ticket base oil lines are fewer, and they are the ones slipping.
04 · Finding two

Two base oil suppliers carry most of the exposure

"Value at risk" in the report is the dollar value of every line that failed OTIF. It totals US$48.3M. When I ranked suppliers by it, the list was very top-heavy.

Supplier A shipped 39 lines worth US$23.7M and only 5 of them were confirmed on time and in full. Supplier B is better at 53%, but its lines are large too. Together they are 5% of lines, 47% of spend and 74% of value at risk.

05 · Finding three

Lines fail on dates, mostly by a week or two

In-full performance is strong at 96%. Of the 560 lines that failed OTIF, 500 failed on timing and only 16 on quantity alone. So the conversation with suppliers is about dates, not volumes.

The late lines are also not wildly late. Almost two thirds of them missed the window by 8 to 14 days.

Some of the lateness starts with us

Every material has a standard lead time (the SLA). I compared the date each buyer requested against that lead time. About one in five lines (424) asked for delivery more than a week faster than the standard. Those lines passed OTIF only 56% of the time.

This changes the ask. Some of the improvement can come from buyers ordering earlier, without a single difficult call to a supplier.
06 · The report

Four pages, from the headline to the line item

The report is built to be read top-down in a weekly meeting: start with the overall score, drop into the supplier that is hurting it, check whether it is a timing issue, then pull the PO lines to chase. Every page shares the same year, month, category and purchasing group filters.

07 · How it's built

One fact table at PO line grain, four lookups

The source was a purchase order export from the ERP, cleaned in Excel and Power Query. I calculated the status flags (on time, in full, SLA comparison) during preparation, so the DAX stays simple and every measure reads from the same 0/1 columns.

The two core measures. The weighted version is the one that changed the conversation:

-- share of PO lines on time and in full
OTIF = DIVIDE ( SUM ( FactPOLine[OTIFFlag] ), [Lines] )

-- same score, each line weighted by its USD value
OTIF(USD) =
DIVIDE (
    SUMX ( FactPOLine, FactPOLine[AmountUSD] * FactPOLine[OTIFFlag] ),
    [Value USD]
)

-- dollar value of every line that failed
Value at Risk (USD) =
CALCULATE ( [Value USD], FactPOLine[OTIFFlag] = 0 )

The model also has month-on-month measures for OTD, In Full and OTIF using DATEADD, a 95% target with a gap-to-target measure, and a supplier rank by value at risk using RANKX over ALLSELECTED, so the ranking respects whatever filters are set.

08 · What I'd do next

Recommendations for the purchasing team

  1. Report OTIF two ways, every month.Keep the line-count score for supplier fairness, and put the value-weighted score next to it so a few large failures can't hide.
  2. Open a recovery plan with Suppliers A and B.If these two performed like the other 37 suppliers do (74% by value), the value-weighted score would rise from 47% to about 74%. Ask for earlier vessel bookings or a rolling forecast so they can plan further out.
  3. Stop requesting dates shorter than the lead time.Flag any PO where the requested date is ahead of SLA before it is sent. If the date really is urgent, call the supplier first instead of finding out from the confirmation.
  4. Fix the missing confirmations.All 44 lines without an ETA come from one forwarder. Make the confirmed date a required field before the PO is marked as acknowledged.
  5. Use page 4 as the chase list.587 lines worth US$24.3M are still open. Filter by supplier and confirmed ETA, and work through it in the weekly call.
What this analysis can't tell you
  • "On time" is based on the supplier's confirmed ETA, not the goods receipt date. It measures whether suppliers commit to our dates, which is an early warning. Actual arrival can still differ.
  • The data covers about eight months of due dates. Some months have only a handful of large base oil lines, so the value-weighted line can swing on a single shipment.
  • Supplier names are anonymised here. The live report shows them because it is the working version.
